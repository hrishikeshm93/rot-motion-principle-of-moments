NCERT Section 6.8.1 • Mechanical Equilibrium of a Lever

6.8.1 Principle of Moments

Why can a small child balance a heavier adult on a playground see-saw, or how does a simple crowbar lift a massive boulder? Let us explore the physics of an ideal lever and discover how forces and their lever arms govern rotational balance.

Core Takeaway

"For a lever in rotational equilibrium, the algebraic sum of moments of forces about the fulcrum is zero:"

Load Arm × Load = Effort Arm × Effort

What is an Ideal Lever?

According to NCERT, an ideal lever is essentially a light (i.e., of negligible mass) rod pivoted at a point along its length. This pivot point is called the fulcrum (O).

Load (F1) & Load Arm (d1)

In a lever, force F1 is usually some weight to be lifted. It acts at point A at a perpendicular distance d1 (the load arm) from the fulcrum O.

Effort (F2) & Effort Arm (d2)

Force F2 is the effort applied at point B to lift the load. Its distance d2 from the fulcrum O is called the effort arm.

Support Reaction (R) at Fulcrum: Since the lever is in mechanical equilibrium, the fulcrum exerts an upward reaction force R directed opposite to F1 and F2. Because R acts right at the fulcrum itself (lever arm = 0), R has zero moment about the fulcrum!

Real-World Examples of Levers (NCERT Activity)

1. Playground See-Saw

Pivoted at the central fulcrum. A lighter child sits farther (d2 > d1) to balance a heavier friend.

2. Beam Balance

Equal arms (d1 = d2). When horizontal, unknown mass equals standard mass (F1 = F2).

3. Crowbar / Bottle Opener

Long effort arm (d2 ≫ d1) gives large Mechanical Advantage to lift heavy loads.

Mathematical Formulation • Equations (6.32a) & (6.32b)

Equations of Equilibrium & Mechanical Advantage

A rigid body in mechanical equilibrium must satisfy both translational equilibrium (ΣF = 0) and rotational equilibrium (Στ = 0). Let us derive the Principle of Moments step by step as presented in NCERT.

Step 1 • Force Balance NCERT Eq. (i)

1. Condition for Translational Equilibrium

Let R be the upward reaction of the support at the fulcrum O. Since R is directed opposite to the downward forces F1 and F2, the net vertical force on the lever must vanish:

R − F₁ − F₂ = 0   ⟹   R = F₁ + F₂   (Eq. i)

Physical Meaning: The pivot or fulcrum must bear the entire combined downward load (F1 + F2) when the ideal massless rod is horizontal.

Step 2 • Torque Balance NCERT Eq. (ii) & (6.32a)

2. Condition for Rotational Equilibrium

Taking moments about the fulcrum O, the sum of moments must be zero. Normally, anticlockwise moments are taken as positive (+) and clockwise moments as negative (−). Note that R acts at the fulcrum itself and has zero moment about O:

d₁F₁ − d₂F₂ = 0   (Eq. ii)
d₁F₁ = d₂F₂   (Eq. 6.32a)
load arm × load = effort arm × effort
NCERT Equation (6.32b)

Mechanical Advantage (M.A.)

From the principle of moments (d1F1 = d2F2), the ratio of the load lifted (F1) to the effort applied (F2) is called the Mechanical Advantage (M.A.):

M.A. = F₁ F₂ = d₂ d₁ (6.32b)
Case 1: Effort Arm > Load Arm (d2 > d1 ⟹ M.A. > 1)

Force multiplier! A small effort (F2) can be used to lift a large load (F1). Example: Crowbar lifting a stone.

Case 2: Effort Arm = Load Arm (d2 = d1 ⟹ M.A. = 1)

Equal forces (F1 = F2). Example: A shopkeeper's beam balance.

Case 3: Effort Arm < Load Arm (d2 < d1 ⟹ M.A. < 1)

Speed/distance multiplier! Requires larger effort than load, such as a human forearm lifting a ball in the palm.

6.8.2 Centre of Gravity (CG)

Have you ever balanced your physics notebook on the tip of your finger? There is one unique point where the entire extended body remains perfectly horizontal without tilting. Let us explore this point—the Centre of Gravity (G)—through two hands-on experiments from NCERT!

Definition of CG

"The Centre of Gravity (CG) of a body is that point where the total gravitational torque on the body is zero."

τ_g = Σ τ_i = Σ r_i × m_i g = 0
NCERT Fig. 6.24

Experiment 1: Balancing an Irregular Cardboard on a Pencil Tip

Take an irregular-shaped cardboard having mass M and a narrow-tipped object like a pencil. By trial and error, you can locate a unique point G where the cardboard remains balanced horizontally.

1. Translational Equilibrium:

The tip of the pencil provides a vertically upward reaction force R equal and opposite to the total weight Mg (R − Mg = 0).

2. Rotational Equilibrium:

Gravity acts on every individual particle (m1g, m2g, …). Point G is so located that the total torque on the cardboard due to all these forces mig is zero! If the pencil tip is moved away from G, an unbalanced torque tilts and topples the cardboard.

Move Pencil Tip Position (Trial & Error): At G (Δx = 0.0 cm)
✓ Balanced at Centre of Gravity G (Στ = 0) Fig. 6.24 Interactive
NCERT Fig. 6.25

Experiment 2: Determining CG of an Irregular Body by Suspension (Plumb-Line Method)

Currently Active View
Step 1: Suspended from Point A → Vertical Line AA₁ Marked

When suspended freely from point A, the cardboard comes to rest such that the vertical line through A passes directly through the Centre of Gravity G. We mark this vertical line as AA₁.

NCERT Think & Answer (Page 113): "Explain why the method works."

Answer: When the body is suspended freely from a point (say A), two forces act on it: the upward tension T at A and the resultant gravitational force Mg acting vertically downward at the Centre of Gravity G. For rotational equilibrium (Στ = 0), the line of action of Mg must pass through the point of suspension A (otherwise Mg would create a restoring torque τ = r_⊥ Mg and rotate the lamina). Hence, G must lie on the vertical line AA₁. Repeating with B and C, the unique intersection of AA₁, BB₁, and CC₁ gives the exact position of G!

NCERT Section 6.8.2 • Equation (6.33) & Conceptual Distinctions

Mathematical Theory of CG & Centre of Gravity vs. Centre of Mass

Are Centre of Gravity (CG) and Centre of Mass (CM) the exact same thing? NCERT explains why they are fundamentally different concepts, and under what condition they coincide!

Vector Derivation NCERT Eq. (6.33)

Gravitational Torque about the CG

Let ri be the position vector of the i-th particle of an extended body with respect to its Centre of Gravity (CG). The torque about the CG due to the force of gravity on the particle is τi = ri × mig.

By definition, the total gravitational torque about the CG is zero:

τg = Σ τi = Σ ri × mig = 0   (6.33)
When does CG coincide with CM?

If the body is sufficiently small, the acceleration due to gravity g is the same for all particles. Hence, g comes out of the summation in Eq. (6.33):

(Σ miri) × g = 0  ⟹  Σ miri = 0  (since g ≠ 0)

Remember that position vectors ri are taken with respect to the CG. By Sec. 6.2 Eq. (6.4a), if Σ miri = 0, the origin must be the Centre of Mass (CM) of the body! Thus, the CG coincides with the CM in uniform gravity or gravity-free space.

Metal Bar on Two Knife-Edges (Fig. 6.26)

g = 9.8 m/s²
Problem 1 (NCERT Ex 6.8): A metal bar 70 cm long and 4.00 kg in mass is supported on two knife-edges placed 10 cm from each end. A 6.00 kg load is suspended at 30 cm from one end. Find the reactions at the knife-edges. (Assume the bar to be of uniform cross section and homogeneous.)
NCERT Fig. 6.26 — Free Body Diagram AB = 70 cm, AG = 35 cm
Interactive Example 6.8 Explorer

Slide the suspended load position AP (from 10 cm to 60 cm) to see how normal reactions R₁ and R₂ shift between the two knife-edges!

Suspended Load Position AP (from end A): 30 cm (PG = 5 cm)
Reaction at K₁ (R₁)
54.88 N (≈ 55 N)
Reaction at K₂ (R₂)
43.12 N (≈ 43 N)
1. Geometry & Forces: Rod is homogeneous & uniform, so CG is at centre G: AB = 70 cm, AG = 35 cm, AP = 30 cm ⟹ PG = 5 cm = 0.05 m. Also AK₁ = BK₂ = 10 cm ⟹ K₁G = K₂G = 25 cm = 0.25 m.
Weight of rod: W = 4.00g N; Suspended load: W₁ = 6.00g N (where g = 9.8 m/s²).
2. Translational Equilibrium (Vertical forces):
R₁ + R₂ − W₁ − W = 0 ⟹ R₁ + R₂ = 4.00g + 6.00g = 10.00g = 98.00 N   (Eq. iii)
3. Rotational Equilibrium (Moments about G, anticlockwise +ve, clockwise -ve):
−R₁(K₁G) + W₁(PG) + R₂(K₂G) = 0   (Eq. ii)
−0.25 R₁ + 0.05 (6.00g) + 0.25 R₂ = 0
R₁ − R₂ = 1.2g N = 1.2 × 9.8 = 11.76 N   (Eq. iv)
4. Solving (iii) and (iv):
R₁ = 54.88 N (≈ 55 N at K₁)   and   R₂ = 43.12 N (≈ 43 N at K₂).

Ladder Leaning Against a Frictionless Wall (Fig. 6.27)

Classic Exam Favourite
Problem 2 (NCERT Ex 6.9): A 3 m long ladder weighing 20 kg leans on a frictionless wall. Its feet rest on the floor 1 m from the wall as shown in Fig. 6.27. Find the reaction forces of the wall and the floor.

What happens as the foot of the 3 m ladder slips farther from the wall (increasing AC)? Watch the required floor friction F = F₁ shoot up!

Wall Force F₁ (= F)
34.6 N

199.0 N

1. Geometry: Ladder length AB = 3 m, foot distance AC = 1 m. By Pythagoras theorem, wall height BC = √(3² − 1²) = √8 = 2√2 m. Weight W = 20 × 9.8 = 196.0 N acts at midpoint D (so AE = 1/2 m).
2. Translational Equilibrium:
Vertical forces: N − W = 0 ⟹ N = 196.0 N   (i)
Horizontal forces: F − F₁ = 0 ⟹ F = F₁   (ii)
3. Rotational Equilibrium (Taking moments about foot A):
2√2 F₁ − (1/2) W = 0   (iii)
F₁ = W / (4√2) = 196.0 / (4√2) = 34.6 N  ⟹  Friction F = 34.6 N
4. Resultant Floor Reaction F₂ & Angle α with horizontal:
F₂ = √(F² + N²) = √(34.6² + 196.0²) = 199.0 N
tan α = N / F = 4√2 ≈ 5.657 ⟹ α = tan⁻¹(4√2) ≈ 80°
Problem 3 • Crowbar Lever & M.A. Section 6.8.1

Lifting a Heavy Boulder with a Crowbar

A uniform light crowbar of total length 1.80 m is used to lift a stone of weight 1200 N placed at one end. If a small pivot stone (fulcrum) is placed 20 cm from the load end, calculate: (a) the Mechanical Advantage (M.A.), (b) the minimum effort required at the other end, and (c) the reaction force on the fulcrum.

• Load arm d₁ = 0.20 m; Effort arm d₂ = 1.80 − 0.20 = 1.60 m.
• (a) Mechanical Advantage: M.A. = d₂ / d₁ = 1.60 / 0.20 = 8.0
• (b) Effort Required (F₂): F₂ = F₁ / M.A. = 1200 N / 8 = 150 N
• (c) Fulcrum Reaction (R): R = F₁ + F₂ = 1200 + 150 = 1350 N
Problem 4 • Finding Unknown Mass & CG NCERT Exercise Style

Metre Stick Balanced with Two Coins

A metre stick is balanced on a knife-edge at its centre (50.0 cm mark). When two coins, each of mass 5.0 g, are put one on top of the other at the 12.0 cm mark, the stick is found to be balanced at 45.0 cm. What is the mass of the metre stick?

• CG of uniform stick is at 50.0 cm. Let mass of stick = M grams.
• Total mass of 2 coins at 12.0 cm mark: m = 2 × 5.0 g = 10.0 g.
• New fulcrum O' is at 45.0 cm.
• Distance of coins from O': d₁ = 45.0 − 12.0 = 33.0 cm (anticlockwise).
• Distance of stick's CG from O': d₂ = 50.0 − 45.0 = 5.0 cm (clockwise).
• By Principle of Moments: 10.0 g × 33.0 = M g × 5.0 ⟹ M = 66.0 g.
Problem 5 • Non-Uniform Rod CG Section 6.8.2

Locating the Centre of Gravity of a Non-Uniform Bar

A non-uniform bar of weight W and length 2.0 m is suspended horizontally by two vertical light strings attached to its ends A and B. The tension in the string at A is 0.60 W and at B is 0.40 W. Find the distance x of the Centre of Gravity (G) from end A.

• Let CG (G) be at distance x from end A. Weight W acts downward at G.
• Taking moments about end A for rotational equilibrium (Στ_A = 0):
• Clockwise moment of W about A = W × x
• Anticlockwise moment of T_B about A = (0.40 W) × 2.0 m
• Equating moments: W x = 0.80 W ⟹ x = 0.80 m (or 80 cm) from end A.
Problem 6 • Critical Tipping Load HOTS / JEE-NEET Bridge

Maximum Overhang Weight Before a Plank Tips Over

A uniform wooden plank of mass 12 kg and length 4.0 m rests horizontally on two supports P and Q. Support P is at one end (0.0 m) and support Q is at 3.0 m from P (leaving a 1.0 m overhang). What is the maximum mass m that can be hung at the extreme overhanging end without tipping the plank?

• CG of uniform 4.0 m plank is at its midpoint: 2.0 m from P (i.e., 1.0 m to the left of Q).
• On the verge of tipping about support Q, the plank just lifts off support P, so normal reaction R_P = 0!
• Taking moments about pivot Q (at 3.0 m mark):
• Anticlockwise moment of plank's weight = (12g) × (3.0 − 2.0) = 12g × 1.0
• Clockwise moment of overhanging mass m = (mg) × (4.0 − 3.0) = mg × 1.0
• Equating moments: mg × 1.0 = 12g × 1.0 ⟹ m_max = 12 kg.
